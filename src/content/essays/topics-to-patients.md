For years, Paper III read like a table of contents. Dementia. Syncope. Immobility. If you had a well-organised essay for each heading, you could reproduce it and move on.

That paper is disappearing. More and more, the examiner builds a patient around the topic and asks you to look after them. A 75-year-old smoker becomes abusive and incoherent on the third day of a fever. A 60-year-old on four culprit drugs keeps falling. A man arrives with a PSA of 10 ng/ml. A woman asks whether she will inherit her father’s Lewy body dementia.

A topic essay can’t answer these. The examiner isn’t checking whether you know delirium. They are checking whether you would recognise it at the bedside, and what you would do next, in the right order.

## Answer the patient in four stages

1. **Differentials.** Name the likely diagnosis, then the alternatives you must exclude.
2. **Investigations.** Choose the tests that separate those possibilities, and say why.
3. **Ward management.** What you would do today, including what you would stop.
4. **Follow-up and counselling.** What happens after discharge, and what you tell the family.

Take the feverish smoker. Stage one is delirium, with the fever’s source as the leading precipitant, while you exclude hypoxia, drug effects, electrolyte disturbance, alcohol withdrawal and stroke. Stage two follows from that list: chest X-ray, blood count, electrolytes, glucose, blood gases if he is breathless, and a look at the drug chart. Stage three treats the cause and the delirium together, with no restraints, and antipsychotics only if he is severely distressed or a danger to himself or others. Stage four is the one most answers forget: checking his cognition after recovery and telling the family what to expect.

## The same shift, for results

A second group of questions hands you something to interpret: a bone density report, a FRAX score, red cell indices, a cardiac enzyme curve, BMI cut-offs for Asians. Recall earns little here. Practise drawing and labelling these until you can do it cold.

## What to do with this

Take each high-yield topic in the index and write it twice: once as a topic, once as a patient. If you can answer both versions, the examiner has nowhere left to hide.
