## Answer at a glance

## Definitions

- **SIRS (1991):** 2 or more of:
  - temperature over 38 °C or under 36 °C;
  - heart rate over 90;
  - respiratory rate over 20, or PaCO₂ under 32 mmHg;
  - white cells over 12,000 or under 4,000, or more than 10% bands.

  It is sensitive but not specific, so it no longer defines sepsis.
- **Sepsis (Sepsis-3, 2016):** life-threatening organ dysfunction from a dysregulated response to infection, meaning a SOFA rise of 2 or more.
- **Septic shock:** vasopressors needed to keep MAP at 65 mmHg, with lactate over 2 mmol/L despite adequate fluids.
- **MODS:** progressive dysfunction of two or more organs that needs intervention.
- **qSOFA:** respiratory rate 22 or more, altered mentation, systolic 100 or below. It flags risk but shouldn’t be used alone to screen.

## Epidemiology

About 49 million cases and 11 million deaths a year worldwide, around a fifth of all deaths. Incidence and death rates rise steeply with age.

## Why older adults

Immunosenescence, multimorbidity, frailty, malnutrition, catheters and devices, aspiration, care-home living, steroids.

## Sources

Pneumonia (the commonest), then urinary tract, abdomen, skin including pressure ulcers, and devices.

## Pathogenesis

1. Microbial and damage signals trigger immune receptors.
2. A cytokine surge follows, together with immune suppression.
3. The endothelium is damaged: capillaries leak, coagulation is activated (DIC), and the microcirculation and mitochondria fail.
4. Organs fail.

## Presentation in older adults

Often atypical: no fever or even hypothermia; new confusion, falls, decline or poor eating. A raised respiratory rate is often the earliest sign. A “normal” blood pressure may be low for a hypertensive person, and beta-blockers hide tachycardia.

## Investigations

- Two sets of blood cultures before antibiotics, if this causes no delay.
- Lactate, blood count, CRP, procalcitonin.
- Renal and liver function, coagulation, blood gas.
- Urine and sputum cultures.
- Imaging to find the source: chest X-ray, ultrasound, CT.

## Management (Surviving Sepsis Campaign 2021)

- **In the first hour:** lactate, cultures, and broad-spectrum antibiotics.
- **Fluids:** 30 mL/kg of balanced crystalloid for hypoperfusion, given as boluses with reassessment in older or cardiac patients.
- **Vasopressors:** noradrenaline first (it can start peripherally), targeting MAP 65 mmHg; add vasopressin if needed, and hydrocortisone if vasopressors continue.
- **Source control:** drain collections, and remove infected lines and catheters.
- **Support:** lung-protective ventilation, glucose 144–180 mg/dL, VTE prophylaxis, nutrition.
- **Antibiotics:** de-escalate with culture results, and use procalcitonin to shorten the course.

## Recent advances

- Balanced crystalloids.
- Capillary refill time and passive leg raise to guide fluids.
- Early peripheral vasopressors, and prolonged beta-lactam infusions.
- Rapid molecular diagnostics and electronic early-warning scores.
- Vitamin C is not recommended; it caused harm in LOVIT.
- In people over 65, a lower MAP target (60–65 mmHg) didn’t worsen mortality (the 65 trial).

## Geriatric care

Prevent delirium, mobilise early, feed well, review medicines, and discuss goals of care early.

## Prognosis

Mortality rises with age and frailty, and septic shock carries a high risk of death. Survivors often have lasting cognitive and functional decline and are frequently readmitted (post-sepsis syndrome).

## One-liner

In an older adult, sepsis may look like nothing more than new confusion, so check lactate and give antibiotics within the hour.
