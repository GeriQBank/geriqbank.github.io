## What it is

Communicating hydrocephalus with large ventricles and normal or intermittently raised CSF pressure. It is either idiopathic (over 60) or secondary (after subarachnoid haemorrhage, meningitis, trauma).

## Triad

- **Gait first and foremost:** magnetic, broad-based, short shuffling steps, trouble turning.
- **Then subcortical cognitive change:** slowness, executive dysfunction, apathy.
- **Then urinary urgency and incontinence.**

## Mechanisms

Impaired CSF absorption, reduced brain compliance, periventricular ischaemia, glymphatic dysfunction.

## Imaging

- **Evans’ index over 0.3.**
- **DESH:** tight high-convexity sulci with enlarged Sylvian fissures.
- **Callosal angle under 90°.**
- Periventricular change; ventricles large out of proportion to atrophy.

## Differentials

Small vessel disease (vascular parkinsonism), Parkinson’s disease, PSP, Alzheimer’s disease, spinal stenosis, neuropathy. These often coexist.

## Predicting response to a shunt

- **High-volume tap test (30–50 mL),** timing gait before and after (timed up-and-go, 10-metre walk).
- External lumbar drainage over 3 days (more sensitive).
- CSF infusion testing.

## Treatment

- **Ventriculoperitoneal shunt with a programmable valve.** Gait improves most in selected patients; cognition less.
- **Complications:** subdural haematoma from overdrainage, infection, blockage, seizures.
- Shunting is reasonable even in the very old if fit, and earlier is better.

## One-liner

Gait first, then memory, then bladder; time the walk before and after a big tap, and shunt the responders.
