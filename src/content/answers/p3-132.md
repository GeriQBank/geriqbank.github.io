## Answer at a glance

1. How GERD differs in older adults.
2. Contributors, including drugs.
3. Alarm features and diagnosis.
4. Management, including PPI deprescribing.

## In older adults

Less classic heartburn but more complications (oesophagitis, stricture, Barrett’s), dysphagia and anaemia.

## Contributors

Hiatus hernia, less saliva, drugs that relax the lower oesophageal sphincter (nitrates, calcium-channel blockers, theophylline, anticholinergics, benzodiazepines). Pill oesophagitis from bisphosphonates, NSAIDs, doxycycline, potassium and iron.

## Alarm features needing endoscopy

Dysphagia, painful swallowing, weight loss, anaemia or bleeding, vomiting, a mass, or **new symptoms at 60 or over**.

## Diagnosis

A clinical diagnosis plus a PPI trial if there are no alarm features. Endoscopy (LA grades A–D), and pH-impedance testing if refractory.

## Management

- Weight loss, head-of-bed elevation, no meals within 2–3 hours of bed, stop smoking, review drugs.
- PPI once daily before breakfast for 8 weeks, then the lowest effective dose or on-demand use.
- Deprescribe long-term PPIs where possible: C. difficile, pneumonia, B12 deficiency, low magnesium and fractures are all risks.
- **Alternatives:** H2 blockers, or vonoprazan for erosive oesophagitis.
- Fundoplication for selected patients.

## One-liner

New dyspepsia after 60 earns an endoscopy, not just a PPI.
