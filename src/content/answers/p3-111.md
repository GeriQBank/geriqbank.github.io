## Answer at a glance

1. What DXA measures, and where.
2. Reading the T-score and Z-score.
3. Pitfalls and follow-up.
4. FRAX: what it gives, its inputs, thresholds and limits.

## What DXA measures

Areal bone mineral density (g/cm²), using two X-ray energies at a low radiation dose.

## Sites

Lumbar spine (L1–L4), total hip and femoral neck. Use the one-third radius when the spine or hip can’t be used, or in hyperparathyroidism.

## T-score (postmenopausal women, and men 50 or older)

- −1.0 or above: normal.
- Between −1.0 and −2.5: low bone mass.
- −2.5 or below: osteoporosis; severe if there is also a fragility fracture.

Report the lowest of spine, total hip and femoral neck.

## Z-score (premenopausal women, men under 50)

−2.0 or below is “below the expected range for age”, so look for secondary causes.

## Pitfalls

Osteophytes, vertebral fractures, aortic calcification and scoliosis falsely raise spine BMD. Exclude the affected vertebrae, keeping at least two. For follow-up, use the same machine and judge change against the least significant change.

## FRAX

The 10-year probability of a hip fracture and of a major osteoporotic fracture (hip, clinical spine, forearm, humerus), for ages 40–90.

- **Inputs:** age, sex, weight, height, previous fragility fracture, parental hip fracture, current smoking, glucocorticoids (5 mg or more of prednisolone daily for 3 months or longer), rheumatoid arthritis, secondary osteoporosis, alcohol (3 or more units a day), and optionally femoral neck BMD.
- Use the country model; India has one.
- A commonly quoted US treatment threshold is hip 3% or more, or major fracture 20% or more.
- **Limits:** FRAX ignores falls, steroid dose and spine BMD.

## One-liner

Report the lowest T-score, and remember that a hip or vertebral fragility fracture means osteoporosis whatever the T-score.
