## Terms

Bereavement is the loss through death; grief is the emotional response; mourning is its social and cultural expression.

## Normal grief

- Waves of sadness and yearning, disturbed sleep and appetite, guilt, anger.
- The **dual process model**: oscillating between facing the loss and rebuilding life. Kübler-Ross’s stages aren’t a fixed sequence.
- Intensity eases over months.

## Older adults

- Repeated losses; the **“widowhood effect”** (higher mortality, especially in the first 6 months).
- Loneliness, loss of role, financial change, carer exhaustion, anticipatory grief.

## Prolonged grief disorder

- DSM-5-TR (12 months or more after the death) and ICD-11 (6 months or more).
- Intense yearning or preoccupation plus other symptoms (identity disruption, disbelief, avoidance, emotional pain, numbness, meaninglessness, loneliness), with impairment.
- Risks: sudden or traumatic death, a dependent relationship, prior depression, poor support.

## Grief versus depression

- In grief, self-esteem is preserved and the pangs centre on the deceased.
- Depression brings pervasive anhedonia, worthlessness and suicidality.
- They can coexist, and depression should then be treated.

## Assess

Prolonged grief, depression (GDS), **suicide risk (high in older widowers)**, alcohol, self-neglect.

## Support

- Normalise grief; practical help; social and peer support; hospice bereavement services. Respect religious and cultural rituals (such as the 13-day observances).
- **No routine sedatives or antidepressants for normal grief.**
- Grief-focused psychotherapy for prolonged grief; antidepressants for comorbid depression.
- Follow-up from the clinician, a condolence letter or call, and attention to physical health.

## One-liner

Grief isn’t an illness, but in an older widower watch for the depression, drink and suicide risk hiding behind it.
