## Answer at a glance

1. Changes in innate immunity.
2. Changes in adaptive immunity.
3. Inflammaging.
4. Clinical consequences, including cancer.
5. Strengthening immunity in older adults.

## Innate immunity

- **Neutrophils:** poorer chemotaxis, phagocytosis and NET formation.
- **Macrophages and dendritic cells:** weaker phagocytosis and antigen presentation.
- **NK cells:** more of them, but less killing per cell.
- Blunted Toll-like receptor signalling.
- **Weaker barriers:** thin skin, poor mucociliary clearance and cough, low stomach acid.

## Adaptive immunity

- The thymus involutes, so there are fewer naive T cells and a narrower repertoire.
- Memory and effector T cells build up, including CD28-null CD8 cells driven by CMV (“memory inflation”). An inverted CD4:CD8 ratio forms the “immune risk profile”.
- **B cells:** fewer naive cells and weaker class switching, so poorer responses to new antigens and vaccines, and more autoantibodies.

## Inflammaging

Chronic low-grade inflammation (IL-6, TNF-α, CRP) from senescent cells and their secretions (SASP), gut dysbiosis, visceral fat and persistent infections. It is linked to frailty, sarcopenia, atherosclerosis and dementia.

## Clinical consequences

- More frequent and more severe infections (pneumonia, UTI, zoster, TB reactivation).
- Atypical presentations, such as a blunted fever.
- Weaker vaccine responses, more autoimmunity, more cancer.

## Immunosenescence and cancer

- Weaker immune surveillance, and a pro-tumour environment created by senescent cells.
- Yet immune checkpoint inhibitors work about as well in older adults as in younger ones.

## Strengthening immunity

- **Vaccines designed for older people:** high-dose or adjuvanted influenza, recombinant zoster, conjugate pneumococcal, RSV.
- Exercise, adequate protein, vitamin D and zinc if deficient, and sleep.
- Avoid unnecessary immunosuppression. Senolytics remain research only.

## One-liner

Older immune systems need better vaccines, not fewer; use high-dose or adjuvanted versions.
