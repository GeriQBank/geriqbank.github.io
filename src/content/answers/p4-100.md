## Definition

Progressive degeneration of upper and lower motor neurons. ALS is the commonest form.

## Epidemiology

About 2 per 100,000 a year; peaks at 55–75 years; slightly commoner in men; about 10% familial.

## Etiopathogenesis

- **Genes:** C9orf72 expansions (the commonest), SOD1, TARDBP, FUS.
- TDP-43 protein aggregates are found in about 97% of cases.
- **Mechanisms:** glutamate excitotoxicity, oxidative stress, protein aggregation, mitochondrial dysfunction, impaired axonal transport, faulty RNA processing, neuroinflammation.

## Clinical types

- **ALS (upper and lower motor neuron signs):**
  - Limb onset (the commonest): asymmetric weakness, wasting, fasciculations and cramps, with spasticity, brisk reflexes and extensor plantars.
  - Bulbar onset: dysarthria and dysphagia; commoner in older women, with a worse prognosis.
- **Progressive bulbar palsy.**
- **Progressive muscular atrophy:** lower motor neuron only, with a better outlook.
- **Primary lateral sclerosis:** upper motor neuron only, slowly progressive.
- **Frontotemporal dementia overlap:** about 15% have frontotemporal dementia, and up to half have some cognitive or behavioural change.
- **Usually spared:** eye movements, sensation and sphincters.

## Other symptoms

Drooling, emotional lability (pseudobulbar affect), breathlessness and orthopnoea, weight loss, fatigue.

## Diagnosis

- Gold Coast criteria (2020): progressive motor impairment with upper and lower motor neuron signs in one region, or lower motor neuron signs in two, after excluding other causes.
- **EMG:** active denervation and chronic reinnervation.
- **Nerve conduction:** normal sensory results; excludes multifocal motor neuropathy.
- **MRI brain and spine,** especially to exclude cervical myelopathy, a common mimic in older adults.
- **Blood tests:** CK, B12, thyroid, calcium and PTH, electrophoresis, anti-GM1 antibodies, HIV.
- Genetic testing if there’s a family history.
- Breathing tests (FVC, sniff nasal pressure).

## Differential diagnosis

Cervical spondylotic myelopathy, multifocal motor neuropathy, inclusion body myositis, Kennedy’s disease, myasthenia (bulbar), hyperthyroidism, post-polio syndrome, paraneoplastic disease.

## Course and prognosis

- Relentless, with median survival of about 2–4 years from onset; about 10% live beyond 10 years.
- It is worse with bulbar onset, older age, early respiratory involvement, rapid weight loss or frontotemporal dementia.
- Death is usually from respiratory failure.

## Treatment

- **Disease-modifying drugs:**
  - Riluzole prolongs survival by a few months; monitor liver function and neutrophils.
  - Edaravone has modest benefit in selected patients.
  - Tofersen is an antisense drug for SOD1-related ALS.
- **Multidisciplinary care,** which itself improves survival and quality of life:
  - Physiotherapy, with splints and ankle-foot orthoses.
  - Occupational therapy.
  - Speech therapy, including communication aids and eye-gaze devices.
  - Dietitian support: a high-calorie diet, and a gastrostomy before FVC falls below 50%.
  - **Non-invasive ventilation** for orthopnoea, hypercapnia or FVC below 50%; it prolongs survival.
- **Symptom control:**
  - Drooling: glycopyrronium, atropine drops, or botulinum toxin to the salivary glands.
  - Cramps: mexiletine; quinine isn’t recommended.
  - Spasticity: baclofen or tizanidine.
  - Emotional lability: dextromethorphan–quinidine or SSRIs.
  - Depression, pain, constipation, and breathlessness (opioids).
- **Plan ahead early:** discuss ventilation, gastrostomy, tracheostomy and end-of-life wishes, and involve palliative care.

## In older adults

Diagnosis is often delayed, with symptoms blamed on stroke, cervical spondylosis or “age”. Bulbar onset is commoner, progression faster, and interventions need careful weighing.

## One-liner

Wasting, fasciculations and brisk reflexes in the same limb, with sensation spared, mean MND until proven otherwise.
