## Definition

A single or repeated act, or a failure to act, by someone in a relationship of trust, that causes harm or distress to an older person (WHO, Toronto Declaration 2002). 15 June is World Elder Abuse Awareness Day.

## Epidemiology

- Worldwide, about 1 in 6 people aged 60 and over experienced some form of abuse in the community in the past year (WHO-led review, 2017).
- Rates are higher in institutions: about 2 in 3 care staff report having committed some abuse in the past year.
- **India:** LASI Wave 1 (2017–18) found about 5% of older adults reporting ill-treatment in the past year, almost certainly an underestimate. Family members, often adult children and their spouses, are the usual perpetrators.
- Most cases are never reported.

## Types

- **Physical:** hitting, pushing, burns, inappropriate physical or chemical restraint (sedatives).
- **Psychological:** threats, humiliation, intimidation, isolation.
- **Financial or material:** theft, fraud, forced property transfer, misuse of pension or power of attorney.
- **Sexual:** any non-consensual sexual contact.
- **Neglect:** withholding food, medicines, hygiene, aids or care.
- Abandonment, and violation of rights (confinement, denying choices).
- Self-neglect is often included in practice.

**Settings:** domestic, institutional (care homes, hospitals) and societal (ageism, discriminatory policies).

## Risk factors

- **The older person:** dementia, physical dependence, poor health, social isolation, low income, previous abuse.
- **The perpetrator:** caregiver stress and burnout, mental illness, alcohol or substance misuse, financial or housing dependence on the older person, a history of violence.
- **The relationship:** shared living, long-standing family conflict.
- **Society:** ageism, weakening joint families, migration of the young, too few services; understaffed, poorly trained institutions.

## Identification: red flags

- **Injuries:** unexplained or repeated injuries; bruises that are large (5 cm or more), patterned, or on the face, neck, ears, inner arms or back; fractures of different ages; burns.
- **Neglect:** malnutrition, dehydration, poor hygiene, untreated pressure ulcers, missing glasses, hearing aids or dentures, over- or under-medication.
- **Behaviour:** fear or withdrawal around the caregiver; a caregiver who answers for the patient or won’t leave them alone; delay in seeking care; explanations that don’t fit the injury; frequent emergency visits; missed appointments.
- **Financial:** unpaid bills despite adequate income, sudden changes to a will or property, missing belongings.
- **Sexual:** genital injury, STIs.

## Screening tools

- **Elder Abuse Suspicion Index (EASI):** 6 questions, for cognitively intact older adults.
- Hwalek–Sengstock Elder Abuse Screening Test.
- Vulnerability to Abuse Screening Scale.
- Elder Assessment Instrument.
- **Caregiver Abuse Screen (CASE),** for caregivers.

## Diagnosis

- Interview the older person **alone**, in their own language, asking directly but gently. Take the caregiver’s account separately and compare the two.
- Examine fully: skin, mouth, nutrition, cognition, and genitals where indicated.
- Assess decision-making capacity and immediate safety.
- Investigate as needed: blood count, renal function, albumin, drug levels, X-rays or a skeletal survey, head imaging.
- **Document exactly:** the person’s own words, a body map, measured photographs (with consent), dates and times. The record may be used legally.
- Exclude mimics: senile purpura, bruising on anticoagulants, genuine falls, osteoporotic fractures.

## Management

- **Safety first:** if the person is in immediate danger, admit them or move them to a place of safety, and involve the police.
- **Respect autonomy:** an older person with capacity may decline help, so offer choices and keep the door open. Without capacity, act in their best interests, by the least restrictive route.
- **Multidisciplinary team:** geriatrician, social worker, nurse, psychologist, police, legal aid, NGOs.
- Treat injuries, undernutrition and pressure ulcers; review medicines and stop any chemical restraint.
- **Address the cause:** caregiver support, respite and day care, counselling, and treatment of the abuser’s substance misuse or mental illness where appropriate.
- **Protect finances:** bank safeguards, and revoke a misused power of attorney.
- Alternative housing (old age homes, shelters) if needed; a safety plan and follow-up.

## Legal framework in India

- **Maintenance and Welfare of Parents and Senior Citizens Act, 2007:**
  - Children and relatives must maintain senior citizens, with Maintenance Tribunals deciding within 90 days.
  - Property transferred on condition of care can be declared void if care isn’t given.
  - Abandoning a senior citizen is punishable with up to 3 months’ imprisonment or a fine of up to ₹5,000.
  - States must provide old age homes and medical care.
- Maintenance of parents under Section 125 CrPC, now Section 144 of the BNSS.
- Protection of Women from Domestic Violence Act, 2005, for older women abused at home.
- Bharatiya Nyaya Sanhita, 2023, for assault, theft, cheating and intimidation.
- National Policy on Older Persons (1999), and the Elderline national helpline, **14567**.

## Role of the social worker

- **Identification:** home visits reveal living conditions, family dynamics and caregiver strain that the clinic misses; they also use screening tools.
- **Assessment:** psychosocial, family and financial assessment; a private interview with the older person; information from neighbours and the community.
- **Planning:** a safety plan, and links to services such as respite, day care, pensions, shelters, legal aid, helplines and NGOs.
- **Advocacy:** liaising with the police, Maintenance Tribunals and courts; helping file applications; making sure the older person’s voice is heard.
- **Counselling:** family counselling and mediation when it’s safe; educating and supporting caregivers.
- **Follow-up and prevention:** monitoring over time, community awareness, and training health staff.

## Prevention

Caregiver support and respite, day-care centres, senior citizens’ groups against isolation, awareness campaigns, financial literacy and safeguards, routine enquiry in health care, and standards for institutions (training, adequate staffing, inspections).

<section class="traps">

## Examiner’s traps

- Accepting “fell down” without checking whether the injury fits the story
- Confronting the suspected abuser before the older person is safe
- Overriding the wishes of a person with capacity

</section>

## One-liner

Ask alone, document exactly, put safety first, and respect the older person’s choices.
