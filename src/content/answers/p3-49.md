## Answer at a glance

1. Assess and grade the stenosis.
2. Who needs revascularisation, and when.
3. Best medical therapy for everyone.
4. Endarterectomy versus stenting in an older patient.
5. Centre standards and asymptomatic stenosis.

## Assessment

Duplex ultrasound first, confirmed with CT or MR angiography; grade stenosis by the NASCET method.

## Symptomatic stenosis

TIA or non-disabling stroke in that territory within 6 months:

- **70–99%:** revascularise.
- **50–69%:** consider it, especially in men and after a recent event.
- **Below 50%:** medical treatment only.
- **Timing:** ideally within 2 weeks of the event.

## Best medical therapy for everyone

Antiplatelets, a high-intensity statin, blood pressure and diabetes control, stopping smoking, exercise.

## Endarterectomy vs stenting, for an 80% symptomatic stenosis

- **Endarterectomy (CEA) is preferred over 70.** Stenting carries more periprocedural strokes in older patients (CREST, ICSS), partly because of tortuous, calcified arches.
- **CEA’s downsides:** more MI, cranial nerve injury, and wound problems.
- **Stenting (CAS) suits a “hostile neck”:** previous surgery or radiotherapy, a high lesion, contralateral nerve palsy, or severe cardiopulmonary disease. It needs dual antiplatelet therapy.
- Transcarotid revascularisation (TCAR) is a newer hybrid option.

## Centre standard

Combined stroke and death risk below 6% for symptomatic patients, and below 3% for asymptomatic ones.

## Asymptomatic stenosis

Best medical therapy for most. Revascularise only selected high-risk patients.

## One-liner

An older patient with 80% symptomatic stenosis needs endarterectomy within 2 weeks, plus best medical therapy.
