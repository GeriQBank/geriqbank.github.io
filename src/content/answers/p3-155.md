## Answer at a glance

1. Etiology.
2. Clinical features and special forms.
3. Complications and diagnosis.
4. Antiviral treatment, dosed for the kidneys.
5. Post-herpetic neuralgia and prevention.

## Etiology

Varicella-zoster virus reactivates from sensory ganglia as cell-mediated immunity falls with age or immunosuppression. About 1 in 3 people get it in their lifetime.

## Clinical features

1–5 days of burning pain or tingling, then a unilateral dermatomal vesicular rash that doesn’t cross the midline (thoracic most often), crusting in 7–10 days.

## Special forms

- **Ophthalmicus (V1):** vesicles on the tip of the nose (Hutchinson’s sign) predict eye involvement.
- **Ramsay Hunt syndrome:** facial palsy with ear vesicles.
- Disseminated zoster in the immunocompromised.

## Complications

Post-herpetic neuralgia (pain beyond 90 days, commoner with age), bacterial superinfection, stroke from vasculopathy, myelitis, motor weakness.

## Diagnosis

Clinical; PCR of vesicle fluid if the picture is atypical.

## Treatment

- Start within 72 hours of the rash, or later if new vesicles or complications appear: aciclovir 800 mg five times daily, valaciclovir 1 g three times daily, or famciclovir 500 mg three times daily, for 7 days.
- Adjust for kidney function, because aciclovir can cause confusion in older adults with CKD.
- Give analgesia, and arrange an urgent eye review for ophthalmicus.

## Post-herpetic neuralgia

Gabapentin or pregabalin (reduce the dose for the kidneys and watch for sedation and falls), lidocaine 5% patch, capsaicin 8% patch; nortriptyline only with caution, as it is anticholinergic.

## Prevention

The recombinant (non-live) zoster vaccine: 2 doses, 2–6 months apart, from age 50, including after a previous episode. It is over 90% effective.

## One-liner

Start the antiviral within 72 hours, dose it for the kidneys, and vaccinate once recovered.
