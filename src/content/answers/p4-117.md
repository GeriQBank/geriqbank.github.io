## Answer at a glance

1. What drug-eluting stents are, and their generations.
2. Advantages over bare-metal stents.
3. Disadvantages.
4. Current practice, especially in older adults.

## What they are

A metal stent coated with a polymer that releases an antiproliferative drug (sirolimus, everolimus, zotarolimus, biolimus), stopping the tissue growth that causes restenosis.

## Generations

- The first (sirolimus, paclitaxel) had more late stent thrombosis.
- Newer stents have thinner struts and biocompatible, biodegradable or no polymers.
- Bioresorbable scaffolds were withdrawn because of higher thrombosis.

## Advantages over bare-metal stents

- Far less restenosis and repeat revascularisation.
- Newer DES also cause less stent thrombosis.
- In older and high-bleeding-risk patients, DES with short dual antiplatelet therapy beat bare-metal stents: the SENIOR trial (ages 75 and over) and LEADERS FREE.

## Disadvantages

Dual antiplatelet therapy means bleeding risk, especially in older adults; late thrombosis with early devices; slower endothelial healing; cost; rare polymer hypersensitivity.

## Current practice

Bare-metal stents are essentially obsolete. DES are recommended for all PCI, including very old and high-bleeding-risk patients, with a 1–3-month antiplatelet course where needed.

## In older adults

Assess bleeding risk (PRECISE-DAPT, ARC-HBR), add a PPI, simplify medicines, and check adherence.

## In-stent restenosis

Treat with a drug-coated balloon or another DES.

## One-liner

Today even the oldest patient gets a drug-eluting stent, just with a shorter course of dual antiplatelets.
