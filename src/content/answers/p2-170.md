## Epidemiology

New-onset epilepsy is commonest in older adults, and incidence climbs steeply after 60.

## Causes

- **Cerebrovascular disease** (30–50%, the commonest), including silent small-vessel disease.
- **Neurodegeneration:** Alzheimer’s raises seizure risk.
- Tumours (primary and metastatic); head injury and subdural haematoma.
- **Acute symptomatic causes:**
  - Low or very high glucose; low sodium or calcium; uraemia.
  - Alcohol withdrawal.
  - Drugs: tramadol, bupropion, antipsychotics, carbapenems and cephalosporins in CKD, theophylline.
- Infection. In about a third, no cause is found.

## Definition (ILAE 2014)

2 unprovoked seizures more than 24 hours apart, **or** 1 unprovoked seizure with at least a 60% recurrence risk (for example after a stroke, or with an epileptiform EEG), or an epilepsy syndrome. So a single seizure after a stroke often counts as epilepsy.

## Presentation in older adults

- Mostly **focal seizures with impaired awareness**: confusion, staring, memory gaps, “funny turns”, falls.
- Post-ictal confusion can last for days.
- Often misdiagnosed as TIA, syncope, dementia or delirium.
- **Non-convulsive status** presents as confusion or coma.

## Differentials

Syncope (the commonest mimic, including convulsive syncope), TIA, transient global amnesia, delirium, hypoglycaemia, REM sleep behaviour disorder, psychogenic attacks.

## Evaluation

- A detailed eyewitness history (a phone video helps).
- ECG for arrhythmia.
- Glucose, sodium, calcium, magnesium, renal and liver function.
- **MRI** (CT acutely).
- **EEG:** less sensitive in older adults, and benign age-related variants are often over-read. Use prolonged or video EEG if unclear, and continuous EEG for suspected non-convulsive status.

## Drug treatment

- Start after a first unprovoked seizure if recurrence risk is high, which is common after a stroke.
- **First choices:**
  - **Lamotrigine:** well tolerated (SANAD II), but titrate slowly because of rash.
  - **Levetiracetam:** few interactions and an IV form; dose for the kidneys and watch mood.
  - Lacosamide (PR prolongation) and brivaracetam are alternatives.
- **Avoid enzyme inducers** (carbamazepine, phenytoin, phenobarbital). They interact with warfarin, DOACs and statins, cause bone loss, and carbamazepine and oxcarbazepine cause low sodium.
- **Valproate** brings tremor, parkinsonism, weight gain, liver toxicity, low platelets and interactions.
- **Principles:** start low and go slow, since lower doses often work. Use one drug, and monitor falls, cognition, mood, sodium and bone health (vitamin D, calcium, DXA).
- **Outcome:** most older adults become seizure-free on a single drug.

## Safety

- Driving restrictions per local law.
- Falls prevention; showers, not baths.
- Pill organisers, and caregiver education.
- A rescue plan with buccal or intranasal midazolam.

## Status epilepticus

1. A benzodiazepine at 5 minutes (IV lorazepam, or IM or buccal midazolam).
2. A second-line drug at 20 minutes: levetiracetam 60 mg/kg (maximum 4.5 g), valproate 40 mg/kg, or fosphenytoin 20 mg PE/kg (ESETT).
3. Anaesthesia in ICU at 40 minutes.

## Psychosocial care

Address stigma, depression and the cognitive effects.

## One-liner

In older adults epilepsy looks like confusion more often than convulsions, and the first choice is levetiracetam or lamotrigine, started low.
