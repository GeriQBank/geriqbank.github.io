## Answer at a glance

1. Causes.
2. Pathophysiology.
3. Symptoms and signs.
4. Investigations, and the echo criteria for severe AS.
5. Management: medical care, and when and how to replace the valve.

## Causes

Calcific degeneration of a normal valve (the commonest over 70), a bicuspid valve (presenting at 50–70), and rheumatic disease (in India, often with mitral disease).

## Pathophysiology

Outflow obstruction leads to LV hypertrophy, diastolic dysfunction and reduced coronary reserve, which cause angina, syncope and heart failure.

## Symptoms

- Exertional breathlessness, angina, exertional syncope, heart failure. In older adults, often just fatigue blamed on age.
- GI bleeding from angiodysplasia (Heyde’s syndrome).
- Once symptoms appear, survival without treatment is only a few years.

## Signs

- A slow-rising, low-volume pulse (which stiff arteries in older adults can mask) and a narrow pulse pressure.
- A heaving apex.
- An ejection systolic murmur at the aortic area radiating to the carotids, peaking later as severity increases.
- A soft or absent A2, and an S4.

## Investigations

- ECG (LVH with strain, conduction block) and chest X-ray.
- **Echo:** severe AS means peak velocity of 4 m/s or more, mean gradient of 40 mmHg or more, and valve area below 1.0 cm².
- Dobutamine echo or CT calcium score for low-flow, low-gradient AS.
- BNP; exercise testing only if asymptomatic.
- Coronary assessment before intervention.

## Management

- No drug slows progression (statins don’t). Treat blood pressure cautiously, and avoid dehydration and vasodilators.
- **Valve replacement** for symptomatic severe AS, or asymptomatic severe AS with EF below 50% or very high velocity.
- **TAVI** is preferred in older or higher-risk patients. Surgery (SAVR) suits younger, low-risk patients. The heart team decides, including a frailty assessment.
- Balloon valvuloplasty as a bridge.

## One-liner

Symptoms in severe AS are a deadline; refer for valve replacement, because age alone doesn’t rule out TAVI.
