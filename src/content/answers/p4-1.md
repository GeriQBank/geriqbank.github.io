## Answer at a glance

1. Classify: programmed, damage/error and evolutionary theories.
2. Explain each group.
3. The modern synthesis: the 12 hallmarks of ageing.
4. Clinical relevance.

## Two broad groups

Programmed theories (ageing follows a biological timetable) and damage or error theories (ageing is accumulated wear). Evolutionary theories explain why ageing exists at all.

## Programmed theories

- **Programmed longevity:** genes switch on and off in sequence.
- **Replicative senescence:** cells divide only a limited number of times (the Hayflick limit) as telomeres shorten.
- **Endocrine:** hormonal clocks. Reduced insulin/IGF-1 signalling extends life in worms, flies and mice.
- **Immunological:** thymic involution and immunosenescence.

## Damage and error theories

- Wear and tear (Weismann).
- Free radicals (Harman, 1956), and the mitochondrial version.
- Rate of living (Pearl).
- **Cross-linking:** glycation end products stiffen collagen and other proteins.
- Somatic mutation and DNA damage.
- Error catastrophe (Orgel).

## Evolutionary theories

- **Mutation accumulation (Medawar):** late-acting harmful genes escape selection.
- **Antagonistic pleiotropy (Williams):** genes that help in youth harm in old age.
- **Disposable soma (Kirkwood):** energy goes to reproduction rather than repair.

## Modern synthesis: the 12 hallmarks of ageing (2023)

Genomic instability, telomere attrition, epigenetic change, loss of proteostasis, disabled autophagy, deregulated nutrient sensing, mitochondrial dysfunction, cellular senescence, stem cell exhaustion, altered intercellular communication, chronic inflammation, and dysbiosis.

## Clinical relevance

These point to interventions under study: calorie restriction, exercise, rapamycin, metformin (the TAME trial) and senolytics. None is yet proven to extend human life.

## One-liner

No single theory explains ageing; the hallmarks bring the damage and programme views together.
