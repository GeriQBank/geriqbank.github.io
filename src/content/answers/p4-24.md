## Definition

Ongoing health and social support for people who, because of lasting loss of capacity, can’t fully care for themselves. The aim is to maintain their function, rights and dignity (WHO).

## Who needs it

People dependent in daily activities because of dementia, stroke, frailty, multimorbidity, severe disability or end-stage disease.

## Settings

- **Home:** family caregivers, paid home care, home nursing.
- **Community:** day-care centres, respite care.
- **Residential:** assisted living, old age homes, nursing homes.
- **Hospital:** long-stay and palliative beds.

## Components

Personal care (bathing, dressing, toileting, feeding), nursing and medical care, rehabilitation, medicines management, nutrition, assistive devices, psychosocial support, end-of-life care, and support for family caregivers.

## The Indian scenario

- The family, mostly women, provides almost all long-term care.
- Formal facilities are few, mostly private or NGO-run, and largely unregulated.
- There is no public long-term care insurance.
- Demand is rising with ageing, migration of the young and nuclear families.
- Public support comes through old age homes and day care under Atal Vayo Abhyuday Yojana, NPHCE home visits, and Kerala’s community palliative network.

## Quality issues

Abuse and neglect in institutions, untrained staff, falls, pressure ulcers, infection, loss of dignity.

## Principles

Person-centred, integrated with health care (WHO ICOPE), focused on maintaining function, supportive of family caregivers, with sustainable financing and enforced standards.

## One-liner

Long-term care is less about beds and more about helping people keep doing what matters to them.
