## Answer at a glance

2. The legal journey: 2011, 2018 and 2023.
3. The current status.

## Definition

Written instructions from a person with capacity about treatment they would or wouldn’t want if they later lose capacity. There are two forms: a **living will** (treatment choices) and a **healthcare proxy** (a person nominated to decide).

## The legal journey

- **Aruna Shanbaug (2011):** the Supreme Court allowed passive euthanasia with High Court approval. Active euthanasia stayed illegal.
- **Common Cause v Union of India (2018):** a five-judge bench recognised the right to die with dignity under Article 21, and made living wills valid with a set procedure.
- **The 2023 simplification:**
  - The directive is signed before two witnesses and attested by a notary or gazetted officer.
  - A primary medical board (the treating doctor plus two experts with at least 5 years’ experience) decides within 48 hours.
  - A secondary board, including a nominee of the district chief medical officer, confirms it within another 48 hours.
  - Directives can be kept in digital health records.
- **Mental Healthcare Act 2017:** separately allows advance directives for mental health treatment, and a nominated representative.
- Draft national guidelines (2024) cover withdrawing life support in terminally ill patients.

## Current status

Legally valid, but very few people make one. Awareness is low among doctors and the public, hospitals rarely have procedures in place, and families often decide instead of the patient.

## The geriatrician’s role

- Start advance care planning early, while the person has capacity.
- Explain the choices: ventilation, dialysis, tube feeding, CPR.
- Record their preferences and nominee, review them when health changes, and involve palliative care.

## One-liner

Since 2018 a living will has been legal in India, and since 2023 a notary is enough; the challenge now is getting people to write one.
