## Answer at a glance

## Classification

- **By duration:** acute, or chronic (persistent) beyond 3 months.
- **By mechanism:** nociceptive (somatic or visceral), neuropathic (peripheral or central), nociplastic (altered pain processing, as in fibromyalgia), or mixed.
- **By cause:** cancer or non-cancer.

## Burden

Persistent pain affects a quarter to a half of older people at home and up to 80% in care homes. Common causes are osteoarthritis, back pain and spinal stenosis, osteoporotic fractures, neuropathy (diabetic, post-herpetic), PMR, cancer and peripheral vascular disease.

## Pathophysiology

- The pain pathway runs through transduction, transmission, modulation and perception, with peripheral and central sensitisation in chronic pain.
- With age, descending inhibition weakens and tolerance falls.
- Pain is often under-reported or atypical, as in silent MI or a painless acute abdomen.

## Evaluation

- History: onset, site, character, radiation, severity, timing and triggers, and the impact on function, mood and sleep.
- **Scales:** numerical rating, verbal descriptor, or the Faces Pain Scale. In dementia, observational scales such as PAINAD or the Abbey Pain Scale.
- Examination; investigations as needed; screen for depression and falls; review drugs, including NSAIDs.

## Non-drug management

Exercise and physiotherapy, heat or cold, TENS, CBT and mindfulness, weight loss, assistive devices, yoga or tai chi, education.

## Drugs

Start low, go slow, and give regularly for persistent pain.

- **Paracetamol first:** a maximum of 3–4 g a day, or 2–3 g if frail, with liver disease or alcohol use.
- **Topical options:** NSAID gels for localised osteoarthritis; capsaicin; lidocaine patches.
- **Oral NSAIDs:** rarely, briefly, and with a PPI. Avoid them in CKD, heart failure, peptic ulcer or anticoagulation.
- **Opioids:** for moderate to severe pain that limits function when other measures fail.
  - Start low, for example morphine 2.5 mg; buprenorphine patches are safer in kidney disease.
  - Tramadol needs caution: serotonin syndrome, hyponatraemia, seizures, hypoglycaemia.
  - Always add a laxative, and watch for sedation, falls and delirium. Avoid pethidine.
- **Neuropathic pain:** gabapentin or pregabalin (dose for the kidneys; watch sedation and falls), or duloxetine (watch sodium). Avoid tricyclics, which are anticholinergic.
- **Specific treatments:** steroids for PMR; bisphosphonates or radiotherapy for bone metastases.
- **Interventional:** joint injections, nerve blocks, epidural injections, radiofrequency ablation.

## The role of palliative care in geriatric practice

- Palliative care improves quality of life for patients and families facing serious illness, by preventing and relieving suffering: physical, psychological, social and spiritual.
- It isn’t only for cancer. Older adults with dementia, heart failure, COPD, CKD or frailty follow long, uncertain declines and need it too.
- **What it offers:** symptom control, honest communication, advance care planning, goals of care, caregiver support, coordinated care, end-of-life and bereavement care.
- **How to deliver it:** alongside disease treatment from early on. Geriatricians provide basic palliative care, with specialist teams for complex cases.
- **In India:** the National Programme for Palliative Care, the Kerala model, and easier morphine access since 2014.

## One-liner

Pain in older adults is under-reported and under-treated; ask about it, measure it (even in dementia), and start low but don’t stop short.
