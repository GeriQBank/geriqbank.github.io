## Definition

A syndrome of symptoms and signs from a structural or functional heart abnormality, with raised natriuretic peptides or congestion.

**Types by ejection fraction:** HFrEF (40% or less), HFmrEF (41–49%), HFpEF (50% or more). HFpEF predominates in the very old, especially women.

## Causes

- Hypertension (the commonest in older adults), ischaemic heart disease, valve disease (AS, MR).
- Cardiomyopathies, including ATTR amyloid.
- AF, diabetes, alcohol, anaemia, thyroid disease, CKD.
- Drugs: NSAIDs, pioglitazone, verapamil or diltiazem in HFrEF.

**Precipitants:** infection, AF, ischaemia, salt, missed medicines, NSAIDs.

## Clinical features

- Breathlessness, orthopnoea, PND, oedema, fatigue.
- In the very old, often atypical: confusion, falls, reduced activity, anorexia, night cough.
- Signs: raised JVP, S3, crackles, oedema.

## Investigations

- NT-proBNP (acute thresholds rise with age, for example 1,800 pg/mL above 75).
- ECG, echo, chest X-ray.
- Blood count, renal function, electrolytes, liver function, TSH.
- Iron studies: iron deficiency is ferritin below 100, or 100–299 with TSAT below 20%.

## HFrEF: the four pillars

- ARNI (or ACE inhibitor or ARB).
- Beta-blocker (bisoprolol, carvedilol, metoprolol succinate, or nebivolol, which was studied in the elderly in SENIORS).
- MRA.
- SGLT2 inhibitor.

Plus loop diuretics for congestion, IV iron for deficiency, and devices individualised.

## HFpEF

- SGLT2 inhibitors (EMPEROR-Preserved, DELIVER), and finerenone (FINEARTS-HF).
- Diuretics for congestion.
- Control blood pressure, AF and ischaemia.
- Semaglutide for obesity-related HFpEF.

## Non-drug care

Moderate salt restriction, daily weights, cardiac rehabilitation, vaccines, no NSAIDs.

## Drug classes: benefits and side effects

- **ACE inhibitor, ARB or ARNI:** fewer deaths and admissions in HFrEF.
  - Side effects: cough (ACE inhibitors), angioedema, hypotension, hyperkalaemia, a rise in creatinine.
  - Leave 36 hours between stopping an ACE inhibitor and starting sacubitril-valsartan.
- **Beta-blockers:** fewer deaths. They can cause bradycardia, heart block and fatigue, or briefly worsen congestion, so start them once the patient is euvolaemic.
- **MRAs** (spironolactone, eplerenone): hyperkalaemia and a fall in kidney function; gynaecomastia with spironolactone. Avoid if potassium is above 5.0 or eGFR is below 30.
- **SGLT2 inhibitors:** volume depletion, genital fungal infections, and euglycaemic DKA, so teach sick-day rules. Expect a small early dip in eGFR.
- **Loop diuretics:** relieve symptoms but don’t prolong life. Watch for dehydration, AKI, low potassium and sodium, gout and urgency incontinence.
- **Others:**
  - Ivabradine: sinus rhythm with heart rate 70 or more despite a beta-blocker.
  - Digoxin: AF rate control and symptoms; toxicity with low potassium or poor kidney function.
  - Hydralazine with isosorbide dinitrate: if RAAS blockers aren’t tolerated.
  - Vericiguat: worsening HFrEF.

## Limits of GDMT in older adults

- **The evidence is thin:** the very old, frail, cognitively impaired and those with CKD were largely excluded from trials. SENIORS is one of the few trials in people over 70.
- **Multimorbidity:** many pills, interactions, and adherence problems with cognitive impairment.
- **Side effects bite harder:**
  - Postural hypotension and falls (RAAS blockers, ARNI, diuretics).
  - Bradycardia and conduction disease (beta-blockers).
  - Hyperkalaemia and falling kidney function (RAAS blockers, MRAs).
  - Volume depletion and AKI (diuretics, SGLT2 inhibitors).
- **Most older patients have HFpEF,** where only SGLT2 inhibitors (and finerenone) have outcome evidence.
- **Goals and cost:** symptoms and independence may matter more than longevity, and ARNIs and SGLT2 inhibitors cost a lot in India.
- **Practical approach:**
  - Prioritise the drugs with the best benefit-to-burden ratio, usually an SGLT2 inhibitor and a beta-blocker.
  - Start low, change one drug at a time, and check kidneys and potassium 1–2 weeks after each change.
  - Cut the diuretic once euvolaemic, and deprescribe what doesn’t help (NSAIDs, alpha-blockers).
  - Low doses of several pillars beat none.

## In the very old

- Do a comprehensive geriatric assessment: frailty, cognition, polypharmacy, orthostatic hypotension, kidneys, potassium.
- Start low and add one drug at a time.
- Avoid over-diuresis, which causes falls and AKI. Use digoxin only at low dose with levels checked.
- A heart failure nurse and home monitoring help; discuss goals of care, and involve palliative care in advanced disease.

## Refractory (advanced) heart failure

- Severe symptoms despite optimal therapy.
- First recheck the diagnosis (amyloid, valve disease), adherence and precipitants.
- Treatment:
  - IV diuretics, then add a thiazide (sequential nephron blockade) with close electrolyte checks.
  - CRT where indicated; inotropes only as palliation.
  - LVAD or transplant is rarely possible in old age.
- Palliative care: opioids for breathlessness, subcutaneous furosemide at home, deactivate ICD shocks, and plan ahead.

## One-liner

In the very old, heart failure is usually HFpEF, and the art lies in balancing congestion against dizziness and the kidneys.
