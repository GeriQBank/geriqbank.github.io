## Answer at a glance

1. Why older adults are prone.
2. How a chronic subdural presents.
3. CT appearances.
4. Management, including reversal of anticoagulation.

## Why older adults

Brain atrophy stretches the bridging veins. Risk factors include minor, often forgotten falls, anticoagulants and antiplatelets, alcohol, coagulopathy and dialysis.

## Presentation (chronic subdural)

Develops over weeks with confusion, drowsiness, falls, gait disturbance, hemiparesis and fluctuating symptoms. It mimics dementia, delirium or stroke. Headache is less common in the elderly.

## CT

A crescent-shaped collection that crosses suture lines. It is bright when acute and dark when chronic. The subacute stage is the same density as brain and easy to miss, so look for effaced sulci and midline shift, or use MRI.

## Management

- **Reverse anticoagulation:**
  - Warfarin: vitamin K plus prothrombin complex concentrate.
  - Dabigatran: idarucizumab.
  - Factor Xa inhibitors: andexanet or PCC.
- Stop antiplatelets.
- **Small and asymptomatic:** observe with repeat scans.
- **Symptomatic or causing mass effect:** burr-hole drainage with a subdural drain, which lowers recurrence.
- Middle meningeal artery embolisation reduces recurrence (2024 trials).
- **Dexamethasone is not recommended**, because it worsened outcomes in Dex-CSDH.
- Recurrence is 10–20%. The decision to restart anticoagulation is individual, usually after a few weeks.

## One-liner

A fluctuating new “dementia” in someone on an anticoagulant is a subdural until the CT says otherwise.
