## Answer at a glance

1. Definitions: type 1 and type 2; acute and chronic.
2. Mechanisms.
3. Why older adults are vulnerable.
4. Clinical features and investigations.
5. Managing type 1.
6. Managing acute type 2, including NIV.

## Definitions

- **Type 1 (hypoxaemic):** PaO₂ below 60 mmHg (8 kPa), with normal or low PaCO₂.
- **Type 2 (hypercapnic):** PaCO₂ above 45–50 mmHg (6–6.5 kPa).
- **Acute, chronic, or acute-on-chronic:** in chronic failure, pH is normal because bicarbonate has risen.

## Mechanisms

- **Type 1:** V/Q mismatch (pneumonia, PE, pulmonary oedema, ARDS), shunt, or diffusion impairment.
- **Type 2:** hypoventilation from reduced drive (opioids, sedatives), neuromuscular disease (MND, myasthenia, GBS), chest wall disease (kyphoscoliosis, obesity hypoventilation), or airways disease (COPD, asthma).

## In older adults

Less reserve, blunted responses to low oxygen and high CO₂, weaker muscles, and sensitivity to sedatives.

## Clinical features

Breathlessness, tachypnoea, cyanosis, confusion. High CO₂ adds headache, flapping tremor, a bounding pulse and drowsiness.

## Investigations

Blood gas, chest X-ray, ECG, bloods, and tests for the cause.

## Type 1 management

- Oxygen to SpO₂ 94–98%, and treat the cause.
- High-flow nasal oxygen; CPAP for cardiogenic oedema.
- Intubate if failing.

## Acute type 2 management

For example, a COPD exacerbation:

- **Controlled oxygen** (24–28% Venturi) to SpO₂ 88–92%.
- Bronchodilators and steroids, with antibiotics if indicated.
- **NIV (BiPAP)** if pH is below 7.35 and PaCO₂ is raised after initial treatment. It reduces deaths and intubation.
- Intubation if NIV fails; ICU input when pH is below 7.25.
- Avoid sedatives, and give naloxone for opioid toxicity.
- Agree ceilings of care in advance, since NIV may be the ceiling for frail patients.

## One-liner

In a CO₂ retainer, aim for 88–92% and reach for NIV early, because too much oxygen can worsen the hypercapnia.
