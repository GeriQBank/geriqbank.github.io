## Answer at a glance

1. Types: pMDI, DPI, soft mist inhaler and nebuliser.
2. Barriers in older adults.
3. pMDI technique.
4. Practice points.

## Pressurised metered-dose inhaler (pMDI)

- Needs actuation timed with a slow, deep breath (3–5 s) and a 10-second breath-hold.
- **A spacer (valved holding chamber)** removes the coordination problem, improves lung deposition and reduces thrush from inhaled steroids.
- A breath-actuated MDI is another option.

## Dry powder inhaler (DPI)

- Breath-actuated, but needs a fast, forceful breath: at least 30 L/min, ideally around 60 L/min. Frail patients or those with severe COPD may not manage it.
- Single-dose capsule devices (Rotahaler, HandiHaler) need dexterity.
- Multi-dose devices: Accuhaler, Turbuhaler, Ellipta.
- Powder can clump in humid climates.

## Soft mist inhaler (Respimat)

A slow mist that needs little coordination and low inspiratory flow.

## Nebuliser

Needs no technique. Useful for those who can’t use handheld devices, or in severe exacerbations, but it is slower, costlier and an infection risk if not cleaned.

## Older-adult barriers

- Cognitive impairment (an MMSE below 24 predicts poor technique), arthritis and weak grip, low inspiratory flow, poor vision, poor coordination.
- Mixing device types confuses patients.

## pMDI technique

Cap off, shake, breathe out fully, seal the lips, press as a slow deep breath begins, hold 10 seconds, wait 30–60 seconds between puffs, rinse the mouth after steroids.

## Practice points

- Most patients make at least one error, so check technique at every visit using teach-back.
- Choose the device by inspiratory flow (an In-Check dial helps), dexterity, cognition and cost.

## One-liner

Match the device to the patient, not the patient to the device, and watch them use it.
