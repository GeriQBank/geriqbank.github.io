## Answer at a glance

1. Definition and subtypes.
2. Prevalence and course.
3. Reversible contributors.
4. Evaluation.
5. Management and follow-up.

## Definition

Petersen, NIA-AA, and DSM-5 mild neurocognitive disorder:

- Cognitive decline noticed by the patient, an informant or a clinician.
- Objective impairment in one or more domains.
- Largely preserved independence, though complex tasks may take more effort.
- Not dementia.

## Subtypes

- **Amnestic** (single or multiple domain): usually prodromal Alzheimer’s.
- **Non-amnestic:** points more to Lewy body, frontotemporal or vascular disease.

## Prevalence

Rises from about 7% at 60–64 to about 25% at 80–84 (AAN 2018).

## Course

- About 10–15% a year progress to dementia in clinic samples.
- A sizeable minority (roughly 15–40%) revert to normal.

## Reversible contributors

Depression, drugs (anticholinergics, benzodiazepines), sleep apnoea, B12 or thyroid deficiency, alcohol, hearing or vision loss, uncontrolled vascular risk factors.

## Evaluation

- Informant history and IADLs.
- MoCA (better than MMSE for MCI), and a mood screen.
- B12, TSH and metabolic bloods; MRI.
- In selected cases, amyloid and tau biomarkers (CSF, PET or blood p-tau217) for prognosis or treatment eligibility.

## Management

- Treat reversible causes and control vascular risk; intensive blood pressure control lowered MCI risk in SPRINT MIND.
- Exercise at least twice a week (AAN), cognitive and social activity, hearing aids, a Mediterranean/MIND-style diet, sleep.
- Cholinesterase inhibitors aren’t recommended; they don’t delay dementia.
- Anti-amyloid antibodies (lecanemab, donanemab) are licensed for MCI due to Alzheimer’s with confirmed amyloid, with ARIA monitoring.
- Plan ahead: finances, advance directives, driving.
- Retest every 6–12 months.

## One-liner

MCI is a crossroads, not dementia; treat what’s reversible, keep brain and body active, and retest yearly.
