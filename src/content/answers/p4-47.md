## Answer at a glance

1. What caregiver burden is, and its risk factors.
2. Consequences and assessment.
3. Management.
4. Apathy: recognition and management.

## Caregiver burden

The physical, emotional, social and financial strain of caring for a dependent older person. The caregiver is often an elderly spouse, or in India a daughter or daughter-in-law.

## Risk factors

Dementia with behavioural symptoms, high dependence, living together, the caregiver’s own illness, poor finances, little family help, a strained relationship.

## Consequences

Depression, anxiety, poor sleep, physical illness, isolation, lost income, a higher risk of elder abuse, earlier care-home admission for the patient, and higher mortality in strained spousal caregivers.

## Assessment

Ask directly (“How are you coping?”); use the Zarit Burden Interview (22 items, with short forms) or the Caregiver Strain Index; screen for depression with the PHQ-9.

## Management

- Education about the illness and what to expect.
- Skills training for behaviour problems, transfers and continence.
- Counselling or CBT, and support groups such as ARDSI for dementia.
- **Respite:** day care, home help, short stays.
- Multicomponent programmes work best, as in REACH II.
- Treat the caregiver’s depression, involve other family members, give financial and legal advice, and plan ahead.

## Apathy

Loss of motivation, meaning reduced interest, initiative and emotional response. It’s common in dementia, stroke and Parkinson’s disease, and exhausting for families.

- It differs from depression: there’s no sadness or guilt.
- Assess it with the Apathy Evaluation Scale or the NPI.
- Manage it with structured, enjoyable routines; teach families that it isn’t laziness; treat any coexisting depression. Cholinesterase inhibitors help modestly, and methylphenidate improved apathy in Alzheimer’s disease in ADMET 2.

## One-liner

Ask the caregiver how they’re doing, because they are the patient’s most important treatment.
