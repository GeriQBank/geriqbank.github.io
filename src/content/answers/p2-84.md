## Definition

Hyponatraemia from inappropriate ADH release or action despite low serum osmolality, in a euvolaemic patient. It’s the commonest cause of euvolaemic hyponatraemia, and often “idiopathic” in the elderly.

## Pathophysiology

- ADH acts on V2 receptors, inserting aquaporin-2 water channels, so water is retained and the blood is diluted.
- Mild volume expansion then causes sodium loss in the urine, which is why urine sodium is high.
- An “escape” mechanism prevents oedema.

## Causes

- **CNS:** stroke, subarachnoid haemorrhage, subdural haematoma, meningitis, encephalitis, head injury, tumours.
- **Lungs:** pneumonia (including Legionella), TB, COPD exacerbations, positive-pressure ventilation.
- **Cancer:** small cell lung cancer (ectopic ADH), head and neck cancers.
- **Drugs:** SSRIs, SNRIs, tricyclics, carbamazepine, oxcarbazepine, valproate, antipsychotics, cyclophosphamide, vincristine, cisplatin, opioids, PPIs, desmopressin.
- **Other:** pain, nausea, after surgery, HIV, idiopathic in the elderly; rarely nephrogenic SIAD.

## Diagnostic criteria

**Essential:**

- Serum osmolality below 275 mOsm/kg.
- Urine osmolality above 100.
- Clinically euvolaemic.
- Urine sodium above 30 mmol/L on normal salt and water intake.
- Normal thyroid and adrenal function, and no recent diuretics.

**Supportive:** low urea and uric acid, fractional urate excretion above 12%, and no correction with 0.9% saline.

## Clinical features

These depend on severity and speed: nausea, headache, confusion, unsteadiness, falls, seizures, coma. Mild chronic cases still cause falls, fractures and attention deficits.

## Differentials

Hypothyroidism, adrenal insufficiency (including secondary), diuretics, cerebral or renal salt wasting (which are hypovolaemic), low solute intake, polydipsia.

## Acute management

- Severe symptoms: 150 mL of 3% saline over 20 minutes, repeated, aiming for a 5 mmol/L rise.
- **Correction limits:** no more than 10 mmol/L in 24 hours (8 if high-risk) and 18 in 48 hours. If overcorrected, re-lower with 5% dextrose, with or without desmopressin.

## Chronic management

- Treat the cause: stop the drug, treat the pneumonia.
- **Fluid restriction** (below 800–1,000 mL/day). It’s likely to fail if urine osmolality is above 500 or the urine-to-serum electrolyte ratio (Furst) is above 1.
- **Oral urea** 15–30 g/day: effective and safe, though it tastes unpleasant.
- Salt tablets with a loop diuretic.
- **Tolvaptan:** start in hospital, monitor sodium for overcorrection, and watch the liver (US limit of 30 days). It’s costly.
- Demeclocycline is rarely used (nephrotoxic).

## In older adults

Fluid restriction is hard to follow and falls are a risk. Review drugs (for example, switch an SSRI to mirtazapine) and monitor sodium regularly.

## One-liner

SIADH is a diagnosis of exclusion; rule out thyroid, adrenal and diuretic causes first, and remember urea is a cheap, effective treatment.
