## Answer at a glance

1. What it is, and why the name misleads.
2. Age-related lung changes.
3. How it differs from COPD emphysema.
4. Why it matters.
5. Management.

## What it is

Age-related enlargement of the air spaces from loss of elastic recoil, **without** the alveolar wall destruction and inflammation of true emphysema. The term is a misnomer; “ageing lung” is better.

## Age-related lung changes

- Loss of elastic fibres (with larger alveolar ducts).
- A stiffer chest wall (calcified cartilages, kyphosis) and weaker respiratory muscles.
- Weaker cough and mucociliary clearance.
- Higher closing volume, causing V/Q mismatch and a lower PaO₂ with age.
- Blunted ventilatory responses to hypoxia and hypercapnia.
- Higher residual volume and FRC, and a slightly lower FEV1/FVC and DLCO.

## How it differs from COPD emphysema

COPD emphysema is caused by smoking, involves alveolar destruction and inflammation, and causes symptoms. It is centrilobular in smokers and panlobular in alpha-1 antitrypsin deficiency.

## Why it matters

- Reduced reserve means vulnerability to pneumonia and to respiratory depression from sedatives and anaesthesia.
- On spirometry, the fixed FEV1/FVC cut-off of 0.70 over-diagnoses COPD in the elderly; use the lower limit of normal.

## Management

There is no specific treatment. Avoid smoking, vaccinate, keep active, use sedatives cautiously, and support lung expansion after surgery.

## One-liner

Senile emphysema is an ageing lung, not a disease; don’t label it COPD on a fixed ratio alone.
