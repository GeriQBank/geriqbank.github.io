## Answer at a glance

1. Define it using the Rome IV criteria.
2. Say why it matters more in older adults.
3. List the causes you would look for, drugs first.
4. Evaluate: history, drug chart, rectal examination, bloods, red flags.
5. Treat in steps: the cause, then habits, then the laxative ladder.
6. Close with impaction and opioid-induced constipation.

## Definition

Rome IV defines functional constipation as at least two of the following for the last three months, with symptoms that began at least six months ago:

- Straining in more than a quarter of bowel movements
- Lumpy or hard stools (Bristol types 1–2) in more than a quarter
- A sense of incomplete evacuation in more than a quarter
- A sense of anorectal blockage in more than a quarter
- Manual help to pass stool in more than a quarter
- Fewer than three spontaneous bowel movements a week

Loose stools are rare without laxatives, and the criteria for irritable bowel syndrome are not met.

## Why it matters more in older adults

It is common, under-reported and easy to miss, because it often presents sideways: as delirium, urinary retention, poor appetite, or “diarrhoea” that is really overflow around impacted stool. The risk rises with frailty, immobility, dementia and care-home living.

## Causes to look for

- **Drugs:** opioids, anticholinergics (tricyclics, oxybutynin), calcium-channel blockers (especially verapamil), iron and calcium supplements, aluminium antacids, diuretics.
- **Metabolic:** hypothyroidism, hypercalcaemia, hypokalaemia, diabetes with autonomic neuropathy.
- **Neurological:** Parkinson’s disease, stroke, dementia, spinal cord disease.
- **Structural:** colorectal cancer, strictures.
- **Everyday:** immobility, low fluid or fibre intake, depression, no privacy or help to reach the toilet.

## Red flags: scope before you call it functional

A new change in bowel habit, weight loss, rectal bleeding, iron-deficiency anaemia, an abdominal or rectal mass, or a family history of colorectal cancer. Any of these needs a colonoscopy first.

## Evaluation

- Bowel diary with the Bristol stool chart
- Full medication review
- Abdominal and digital rectal examination: impaction, anal tone, dyssynergia
- Blood count, calcium, TSH, glucose, creatinine and electrolytes
- Plain abdominal X-ray if impaction or obstruction is suspected
- Anorectal manometry with a balloon expulsion test if a defecation disorder is suspected

## The management ladder

1. Treat the cause, and stop or swap constipating drugs.
2. Fix the routine: the toilet after breakfast to use the gastrocolic reflex, privacy, a footstool to raise the knees, more movement, enough fluid and fibre.
3. Laxatives in steps. In frail or immobile patients, start with an osmotic such as macrogol (PEG); lactulose works but bloats. Add a stimulant such as senna or bisacodyl if stools are soft but hard to pass. Keep bulk-forming agents like ispaghula for mobile patients who drink enough, as otherwise they can worsen impaction.
4. For refractory cases, prucalopride or a secretagogue such as linaclotide or lubiprostone, and biofeedback for dyssynergic defecation.

## Special situations

- **Opioid-induced:** prescribe a laxative with every opioid. If that fails, use a peripherally acting opioid antagonist such as methylnaltrexone or naloxegol.
- **Faecal impaction:** clear it from below with suppositories or enemas, with manual evacuation if needed, or use high-dose oral macrogol if the stool is soft. Then start maintenance so it doesn’t recur.
- **Cautions:** avoid magnesium salts and phosphate enemas in renal impairment, and liquid paraffin in anyone at risk of aspiration.

<section class="traps">

## Examiner’s traps

- Starting ispaghula in a bed-bound patient
- Calling it diarrhoea when it is overflow
- Skipping the drug chart, where the cause often is
- Calling it functional without a red-flag screen

</section>

## One-liners

- Macrogol is the usual first laxative in frail older adults.
- Every opioid prescription needs a laxative.
- The rectal examination is part of the workup, not an optional extra.
